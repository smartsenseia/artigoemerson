\begin{abstract}
A transient model for hollow-fiber direct contact membrane distillation (DCMD)
modules with dual heat recovery and partial recirculation of the retentate stream
is employed to evaluate a desalination plant in six modes of operation---batch,
semi-batch, volume-control, salinity-control, continuous, and steady state---focusing
on applications in sites under hydrological stress, such as the semi-arid Brazilian
northeastern region. Adding a second heat exchanger (HX-2) that recovers thermal
energy from the hot retentate before its rejection raises the averaged gain output
ratio in batch mode from $\overline{\mathrm{GOR}}\approx 0.56$ (no heat recovery)
to $\overline{\mathrm{GOR}}\approx 1.82$ (with both HX-1 and HX-2 active), while
lowering the averaged specific thermal energy consumption from
$\overline{\mathrm{SEC}}_{th}\approx 1.24$ to $\approx 0.39$~kWh/L. This gain
originates from the sensible enthalpy still carried by the retentate stream at
the module outlet, which would otherwise be discarded together with the
concentrated brine, and is most pronounced under feed-limited (FLR) conditions
with the mass-flow-rate ratio $\alpha$ close to its critical value $\alpha^{*}$,
since only in this regime does the distillate stream possess sufficient
heat-capacity flow rate to sustain the maximum attainable evaporation of the feed.
Over an 8-h simulation, batch operation yields the lowest specific thermal energy
consumption ($\overline{\mathrm{SEC}}_{th}\approx 0.39$--$0.43$~kWh/L) because the
tank is emptied and refilled sequentially, keeping the mean feed salinity---and
thus the transmembrane vapor-pressure driving force---favorable for most of each
cycle. Semi-batch operation, in contrast, delivers the highest water recovery
ratio ($\mathrm{RR}\approx 76$--$92\%$, against only $2$--$5\%$ in the pure
steady-state mode without recirculation), since fresh brackish water is
continuously supplied at the same rate at which distillate is produced and no
volume is wasted during flush-and-refill transients; the volume-control mode
delivers an intermediate compromise ($\mathrm{RR}\approx 76\%$ at
$\overline{\mathrm{SEC}}_{th}\approx 0.40$~kWh/L for
$C_{s,\max}=120$~g$_s$/L$_{mix}$). Hence, if the primary interest is minimizing
thermal energy cost, batch is the best option; in regions subject to seasonal
hydrological stress, the semi-batch mode is preferable, provided that the
operational costs of pumps and control devices are subsequently included in a
broader thermoeconomic analysis.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Desalination; DCMD system; heat recovery; retentate
recirculation; energy efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Freshwater scarcity, mainly due to population growth, has attracted the attention of
researchers and industry for the development and improvement of techniques for the
treatment, purification, and desalination of salt water. Among the main desalination
techniques, those involving thermal separation methods, such as membrane distillation,
require more energy due to the need to vaporize the water since the water vaporization
enthalpy is about 2{,}400~kJ/kg \citep{Sharqawy2010}, while Gibbs free energy for
seawater, which governs the energy use of the reverse osmosis method, is 2.7~kJ/kg
\citep{Elimelech2011}. Therefore, one of the challenges that must be overcome for the
application of thermal methods such as membrane distillation (MD) in water desalination,
which aims to be competitive against the conventionally employed reverse osmosis (RO)
process in small and medium-sized systems ($<100$~m$^3$/day of water produced), is to
minimize the demand for thermal energy, improving thermal efficiency.

The membrane distillation process enables the use of residual heat from other thermal
processes or the heat available from renewable sources, such as solar collectors.
However, heat losses by conduction through the membrane further increase the thermal
consumption, which, when added to high-water consumption, limits the large-scale use of
these systems \citep{Khayet2011}. Nevertheless, according to \citet{Guan2015}, when
compared to conventional desalination processes, such as multi-stage flash distillation,
multi-effect distillation, and reverse osmosis, the MD process has several benefits:
low salinity sensitivity and high salt rejection (100\% content, theoretically), low
membrane vulnerability to fouling, greater performance under low severity operating
conditions, and the viability of using low-grade waste heat from other thermal processes
or renewable sources, such as solar collectors, as previously mentioned
\citep{Curcio2005, Charcosset2009, Drioli2015}.

Concerning its technology, according to the method of obtaining the vapor pressure
gradient through the membrane, the MD is classified in the following configurations
\citep{Zhang2015, Swaminathan2018}: DCMD (Direct Contact Membrane Distillation), AGMD
(Air Gap Membrane Distillation), SGMD (Sweeping Gas Membrane Distillation), VMD
(Vacuum Membrane Distillation), PGMD (Permeate Gap Membrane Distillation), CGMD
(Conductive Gap Membrane Distillation), V-AGMD (Vacuum-Enhanced Air Gap Membrane
Distillation), among other variants. Each configuration has its advantages and intrinsic
disadvantages, so the choice of one of them is still a research theme, which is usually
directed through metrics such as GOR (Gained Output Ratio) and SEC (Specific Energy
Consumption), which are parameters for energy efficiency analysis. The DCMD is the most
studied and straightforward configuration among them, widely used for desalination
studies and concentration of aqueous solutions.

In general, the increase in the thermal efficiency of MD systems is achieved by improving
the performance of membranes (by choosing membranes with low thermal conductivity,
adequate porosity, pore size and pore size distribution, and thickness), as well as by
reducing the amount of external heat to be supplied to the process
\citep{Gostoli1998, Khayet2006, AlObaidani2008, Yao2013, Li2014, Swaminathan2018}.

The heat supplied to the process can then be significantly reduced by the reuse of the
heat available in the output currents of the desalination module through external heat
exchangers, extending the applicability of the MD process and improving energy efficiency
\citep{Gilron2007, Lee2011, Hausmann2012, Lu2012, Zhang2015, GonzalezBravo2015,
Kim2015, Maheswari2015, Lin2014, Gustafson2018, Christie2020, Lisboa2021, Sampaio2022}.
Obviously, operating conditions such as temperature, the feed and distillate flow rates,
salinity of the feed stream, and its recirculation, among others, significantly affect
the MD process and can improve or reduce process efficiency
\citep{Khayet2000, Wang2009, Zhang2010, Fan2012, Alkhudhiri2012, Guan2014}.

Additionally, since MD presents a low recovery ratio per pass (ratio between the
production rate of pure water and the feed flow), using some form of recirculation of
the brine/retentate stream is mandatory to increase efficiency. In this way, by
considering a CGMD module, \citet{SwaminathanLienhard2018} describe four operation
strategies that enable high global recovery using a single-pass low recovery process
(batch recirculation, semi-batch recirculation, continuous recirculation, and continuous
multi-stage recirculation). The first two operations are classified as
transient/discontinuous processes, while the last two are steady state ones. According
to these authors, who studied the salinity variation from a lower limit of 70~g/kg to an
upper limit of 260~g/kg, the batch operation is the best option since GOR is about twice
for the same distillate flow when compared to the continuous recirculation operation.
In their analyses, using a tank with a high volume to minimize salinity variation, they
adopted a quasi-permanent approach, using a steady state MD model for instantaneous
performance evaluation so that transient effects between each cycle could be ignored. In
this respect, cycling time is thus considered to be very small.

\citet{Sampaio2022} modeled a seawater desalination DCMD plant with heat recovery of
the recirculating hot distillate stream by using a DCMD module composed by a bundle of
hollow-fiber tubes. He combined analytical solutions for the mass transfer problem
across the membranes with the finite volume method for solution the steady-state heat
transfer problem. Still, based on \citet{Christie2020} and \citet{Lin2014}, and to
extend their works, \citet{Sampaio2022} developed an approximation for the optimal
value of the ratio between permeate and feed flow rates, and for the maximum mass
recovery rate that, differently from the expressions developed by \citet{Christie2020}
and \citet{Lin2014}, take into consideration the membrane thermal efficiency.

Thus, based on the works of \citet{Swaminathan2018}, \citet{Christie2020},
\citet{Lisboa2021}, and \citet{Sampaio2022} and extending their analyses, the present
work aims at the development of physical-mathematical modeling, in the unsteady-state
regime, and subsequent numerical simulation of a hollow-fiber DCMD system with double
recirculation and heat recovery. The main goal is firstly to evaluate the potential of
heat recovery from the retentate stream to increase the energy efficiency of the system
to produce distilled water. Furthermore, an application of the model to study the
desalination process in regions with high seasonal hydrological stress, such as the
semi-arid region of the Brazilian northeastern. In addition to the classical batch,
semi-batch, continuous, and steady-state modes of operation, two intermediary modes,
namely, mixing tank volume controlling and mixing tank salt concentration controlling,
are also introduced. The aim is to verify their effect on the behavior of the system
efficiency and on the water recovery ratio (for minimizing the well brackish water
consumption). These controlling schemes can also be operated in conjunction with total or
partial recirculation, i.e., when the brine is rejected from the system with fraction
rejections equal or different to the unity, respectively.

\section{System Description}

The MD desalination plant modelled in the present work is characterized by a water
desalination system that operates in the transient regime, containing a DCMD module (a
vessel with a bundle of hollow fiber membranes), two tanks (a mixing and collector), and
running with dual recirculation and dual heat recovery, where the heat available at the
output of the module from the feed and distillate streams are recovered through the heat
exchangers, HX-1 and HX-2, respectively, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:system}. The heat
source and sink are constant-temperature heat reservoirs, so the feed and distillate
streams reach the specified values of the source/sink temperatures by supplying/extracting
exactly the corresponding amount of heat required. This assumption is adopted for system
evaluation purposes only since, in practice, with the use of heat exchangers,
temperatures are not kept constant, but rather come from sensible waste heat source.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{IMAGENS/1.jpg}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the DCMD system with double heat recovery and partial or total recirculation of the retentate stream.}
    \label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

At the initial time, the feed circuit is completely filled with brackish water at the
well salinity ($c_s^{well}$) and ambient temperature ($T_{amb}$). The distillate circuit
is filled with distilled water at the same ambient temperature. The feed stream leaves
the mixing tank at state~3 ($c_{s3}$, $V_3 = V_{feed}$, $T_3$) and enters HX-1, which
heats it to state~4 ($c_{s4}$, $V_4$, $T_4$) by receiving energy from the distillate
leaving the DCMD module at state~11 ($c_{s11}$, $V_{11}$, $T_{11}$). The mandatory use
of this heat exchanger (HX-1) greatly increases the efficiency of the MD system by
reducing the amount of heat ($q_h$) that must be supplied by the heater to raise the feed
stream temperature to the working temperature at state~5 ($c_{s5}$, $V_5$, $T_5 =
T_{feed}$).

The concentrated module effluent (the retentate) at state~6 ($c_{s6}$, $V_6$, $T_6$)
can be removed from the system (as a reject stream) or it can be recycled (recirculated).
In the present work, the computational code was built taking into consideration both
possibilities, simultaneously or not, depending on a flow rejection fraction ($\lambda$)
at state~9 ($c_{s9}$, $V_9$, $T_9$), which directly and dynamically impacts the
concentration of the solution in the tank and its volume, $V_{mix}$. When $\lambda = 1$,
the brine is completely rejected at state~9 (the MD system must be continuously fed from
the well on a permanent basis). Otherwise, when $\lambda < 1$, the retentate is partially
recycled/rejected. Under this condition, unlike common practice, where a steady-state
approach is employed for long duration operations, a fully transient operation/simulation
must be adopted since the volume and concentration of the mixing tank vary dynamically
with time.

When necessary, depending on salinity and volume of the mixing tank (or the operating
regime), brackish water can be pumped from the well at state~1 ($V_{well}$, $c_s^{well}$,
$T_{well}$), passing through HX-2 as a cold stream to gain energy carried by the effluent
retentate from the DCMD module at state~6, which will be cooled down to state~7 ($c_{s7}$,
$Q_7$, $T_7$). The stream at state~7 can be directed, either totally ($\lambda = 0$) or
partially ($0 < \lambda < 1$), to the mixing tank through state~8 ($c_{s8}$, $V_8$, $T_8$),
to be mixed with the heated brackish water stream coming from state~2.

Thus, in addition to the desalination-system operating modes, batch,
semi-batch, volume- or salinity-controlled, continuous, and steady-state
operation, the numerical simulation will be used to investigate the energy
efficiency of the system when an additional heat exchanger, HX-2, is employed.
This heat exchanger enables thermal exchange between the retentate stream and
the brackish water supplied by the well. The simulation will also evaluate the
partial rejection or recirculation of the retentate stream after heat recovery.

To extend the scope of the simulation, a parametric analysis will be performed
to evaluate the interaction between the NaCl concentration in the feed stream
and the amount of heat recovered from the fluid returning through the feed
circuit of the desalination module. This analysis will also support the
development of operational control strategies aimed at maximizing desalination
efficiency while reducing brackish-water consumption from the well. To perform the present study, 
the operational ranges of the PLICA-7 DCMD module produced by Aquastill for experiments in a relevant 
environment are adopted. Thus, the computational code developed will seek to predict the situations
that can happen in experiments with the pilot unit purchased from Aquastill, from small
modifications in the installed system, or directly in the laboratory scale experimental
module developed in LabMEMS/UFRJ.

\section{Mathematical Model}

The implemented mathematical model incorporates heat and mass transfer in all system
components: mixing tank, distillate tank, DCMD module, heat source and sink, and heat
exchangers 1 and 2. For the DCMD module, the model proposed by \citet{Lisboa2021} was
extended to the unsteady-state regime. The authors treated the hollow-fiber DCMD module
as an equivalent porous medium composed of the following constituents: the porous shell
medium (where the feed stream flows), the porous lumen medium (where the distillate
stream flows), and the membrane (where the distillation process occurs). The membrane is
modeled separately and is not included in the porous medium model. The counterflow
configuration for the flow in the hollow fiber is adopted in the present work. An
illustrative figure of the porous media model employed to analyze the heat and mass
transfer in the vessel is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:porousmedia}.

Here, $\varepsilon_\gamma$ is the porosity of phase $\gamma$ (with $\gamma=l$ for the
lumen phase and $\gamma=s$ for the shell phase), $N_{fbr}$ is the number of fibers in
the vessel, $r_\gamma$ is the radius of the $\gamma$ phase, and $r_v$ is the radius of
the vessel. Given that the membrane has a thickness $\delta$, the outer radius of the
membrane will be $r_s=r_l+\delta$. In the present model, the shell radius,
$r_c = r_v/\sqrt{N_{fbr}}$, is an idealization in the simulations for the outer radius of
a single membrane of the hollow-fiber module (Figure~\ref{fig:fiber}).

The specific surface area, $a_\gamma$, used in heat and mass transfer calculations, is
the ratio of the surface area available for transport phenomena in each phase to the
vessel volume:

\begin{equation}

\label{eq:specsurf}
\end{equation}

The physical model for the control volume of the mixing tank considers two inlet streams
(retentate stream (8), and brackish well water stream (2)) and the tank outlet stream (3).
To recover the heat from the retentate stream, it would be possible to mix it with the
well brackish water directly in the mixing tank, and in order to control the salinity in
the tank, part of the mixture would be rejected immediately afterwards. This would be the
most energetically efficient option, however, some of the brackish water from the well
would then be rejected in this process, which is undesirable due to the hydrological
stress in arid and semi-arid regions.

Therefore, to avoid this problem, and still recover a large part of the thermal potential
of the circulation stream, it is proposed to use an additional heat exchanger (HX-2)
before the rejection process (as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system}), which can be
partial or not. Thus, the mixing tank (and therefore all the desalination system) will be
a dynamic system, since the volume, $V_{mix}$, the concentration, $c_{s,mix}$ (or
salinity, $\phi_{mix}$) and the temperature, $T_{mix}$, of the mixture inside it change
over time.

Considering that the heat exchangers, the distillate and mixing tanks, and the DCMD
module are adiabatic, and that the salinity and temperature fields inside them are
uniform (except inside the DCMD module), the physical situation is mathematically
modelled as follows.

\subsection{Modeling Mass Transfer in the Membrane}
\label{subsec:membrane-mass-transfer}

To partially close the system of equations, the mass-transfer process inside
the DCMD module must be modeled to determine the water-vapor mass flux through
the membrane, $j_{wl}$. Following the procedure adopted by
\citet{Lisboa2021}, the Dusty Gas Model proposed by
\citet{LawsonLloyd1997}, one of the most widely used models for predicting
distilled-water transport through porous membranes, is employed. The molar transport of water 
vapor through the membrane pores is described by

\begin{equation}
\frac{N_w}{D_w^k}
+
\frac{
p_aN_w-p_wN_a
}{
D_{wa}^{0}
}
=
-\frac{1}{RT_m}
\nabla p_w,
\label{eq:dusty-gas-model}
\end{equation}

where $N_w$ is the molar flux of water vapor, $N_a$ is the molar flux of air,
$p_a$ is the partial pressure of air inside the membrane pores, $p_w$ is the
partial pressure of water vapor, $D_w^k$ is the Knudsen diffusivity of water
vapor, $D_{wa}^{0}$ is the molecular diffusivity of water vapor in air, $R$ is
the universal gas constant, and $T_m$ is the average thermodynamic temperature
at each axial position along the membrane. Air is assumed to remain stationary 
inside the membrane pores, such that

\begin{equation}
N_a
\approx
0.
\label{eq:stationary-air}
\end{equation}

Applying Dalton's law, the molar flux of water vapor through the hollow-fiber
membrane, based on the inner membrane radius, is given by

\begin{equation}
N_{wl}
=
\frac{
D_{wa}^{0}
}{
RT_mr_l\ln\left(r_s/r_l\right)
}
\ln
\left[
\frac{
D_{wa}^{0}-D_{\mathrm{eff}}p_s
}{
D_{wa}^{0}-D_{\mathrm{eff}}p_l
}
\right].
\label{eq:water-molar-flux}
\end{equation}

The corresponding water-vapor mass flux is

\begin{equation}
j_{wl}
=
M_wN_{wl},
\label{eq:water-mass-flux}
\end{equation}

where $M_w$ is the molar mass of water.

The molecular diffusivity of water vapor in air is calculated from

\begin{equation}
D_{wa}^{0}
=
4.46\times10^{-6}
\frac{\varepsilon_m}{\tau_m}
T_m^{2.334}.
\label{eq:water-air-diffusivity}
\end{equation}

The Knudsen diffusivity of water vapor is given by

\begin{equation}
D_w^k
=
\frac{\varepsilon_m}{\tau_m}
\frac{d_p}{3}
\sqrt{
\frac{8RT_m}{\pi M_w}
}.
\label{eq:knudsen-diffusivity}
\end{equation}

The effective diffusivity resulting from the combined molecular and Knudsen
transport mechanisms is

\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{eff}}
=
\frac{
D_w^kD_{wa}^{0}
}{
D_{wa}^{0}+PD_w^k
}.
\label{eq:effective-diffusivity}
\end{equation}

The total pressure inside the membrane pores, $P$, is assumed to be equal to
atmospheric pressure. The water-vapor partial pressures at the shell-side and
lumen-side membrane interfaces, $p_s$ and $p_l$, respectively, are calculated
from the corresponding saturation vapor pressures. The effect of salinity is
included through the salt mole fraction, $x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}$, and the
water activity coefficient, $a_\gamma$, according to

\begin{equation}
p_\gamma
=
\left(
1-x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}
\right)
a_\gamma p_{v,\gamma},
\qquad
\gamma\in\{s,l\},
\label{eq:interfacial-vapor-pressure}
\end{equation}

where $p_{v,\gamma}$ is the saturation vapor pressure evaluated at the
corresponding membrane-interface temperature using the Antoine equation. The activity 
coefficient is calculated as

\begin{equation}
a_\gamma
=
1
-
0.5x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}
-
10x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}^{2}.
\label{eq:water-activity-coefficient}
\end{equation}

The morphological characteristics of the membrane are included in the
calculation of the distillate mass flux through the mean pore diameter, $d_p$,
porosity, $\varepsilon_m$, and tortuosity, $\tau_m$.

Models for the effective membrane thermal conductivity,
$\overline{k}_m$, and the membrane tortuosity, $\tau_m$, must also be selected
to close the governing equations. The most common effective-thermal-conductivity
models are based on different geometrical arrangements of the solid and gaseous
phases. In the parallel model, the solid and gaseous phases are arranged in
parallel:

\begin{equation}
\overline{k}_m
=
\left(
1-\varepsilon_m
\right)
\overline{k}_s
+
\varepsilon_m\overline{k}_f,
\qquad
\text{parallel model}.
\label{eq:parallel-conductivity}
\end{equation}

In the series model, the two phases are arranged in series:

\begin{equation}
\overline{k}_m
=
\left[
\frac{
1-\varepsilon_m
}{
\overline{k}_s
}
+
\frac{
\varepsilon_m
}{
\overline{k}_f
}
\right]^{-1},
\qquad
\text{series model}.
\label{eq:series-conductivity}
\end{equation}

For porous media with porosity greater than approximately 0.6,
\citet{GarciaPayoIzquierdoGil2004} recommend the Maxwell I model. This model
assumes that the polymeric matrix consists of a random distribution of solid
particles that do not interact with one another within a homogeneous fluid
medium. The resulting effective conductivity is

\begin{equation}
\overline{k}_m
=
\overline{k}_f
\left[
\frac{
1+2\left(1-\varepsilon_m\right)b_{sf}
}{
1-\left(1-\varepsilon_m\right)b_{sf}
}
\right],
\qquad
\text{Maxwell I model},
\label{eq:maxwell-conductivity}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
b_{sf}
=
\frac{
\overline{k}_s-\overline{k}_f
}{
\overline{k}_s+2\overline{k}_f
}.
\label{eq:maxwell-conductivity-ratio}
\end{equation}

The manner in which the pores and solid matrix are distributed within the
membrane governs the diffusion path and, consequently, the vapor flux.
Membrane tortuosity, defined as the ratio between the actual diffusion path
length and the membrane thickness, is therefore an important parameter for
predicting the transmembrane mass flux.

Several tortuosity models are available in the literature
\citep{Ghanbarian2013}. \citet{Kim2021} analyzed eleven tortuosity models,
including eight geometrical models based on Euclidean structures, such as
cubes, spheres, and cylinders, and three models based on the fractal theory of
porous media. For the geometrical model proposed by \citet{MackieMeares1955}, the membrane
tortuosity is

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{
\left(
2-\varepsilon_m
\right)^2
}{
\varepsilon_m
}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-mackie-meares}
\end{equation}

The model proposed by \citet{Beeckman1990} is

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{
\varepsilon_m
}{
1-
\left(
1-\varepsilon_m
\right)^{1/3}
}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-beeckman}
\end{equation}

For the fractal model proposed by \citet{EliasKohav1991}, the tortuosity is

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{1}{\varepsilon_m}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-elias-kohav}
\end{equation}

The Bruggeman model is given by

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_m}}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-bruggeman}
\end{equation}

According to \citep{Curcino2022}, who evaluated several effective-thermal-
conductivity and tortuosity models for membrane-distillation simulations in
flat-sheet and hollow-fiber configurations, an appropriate model should be
selected based on the known morphological and structural properties of the
membrane employed in the experimental desalination module.


\subsection{Efficiency Metrics}
\label{subsec:efficiency-metrics}

To evaluate the desalination system and quantify improvements in the energy
efficiency of the membrane-distillation process, the thermal efficiency,
$\eta_{th}$, gain-output ratio, $\mathrm{GOR}$, specific thermal energy
consumption, $\mathrm{SEC}_{th}$, and water recovery ratio, $\mathrm{RR}$, are
calculated under the different operating scenarios. The thermal efficiency of 
the membrane is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\eta_{th}
=
\frac{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}
}{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{memb}}
}.
\label{eq:thermal-efficiency}
\end{equation}

The gain-output ratio is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{GOR}
=
\frac{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}
}{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{ext}}
}.
\label{eq:gor}
\end{equation}

The specific thermal energy consumption is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SEC}_{th}
=
\frac{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{ext}}
}{
\dot{V}_{jwl}
}.
\label{eq:sec-thermal}
\end{equation}

The water recovery ratio is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RR}
=
\frac{
\dot{m}_{\mathrm{dist}}
}{
\dot{m}_{\mathrm{well}}
}.
\label{eq:water-recovery-ratio}
\end{equation}

The total heat-transfer rate through the membrane is the sum of the conductive
and latent heat-transfer rates:

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{memb}}
=
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{cond}}
+
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}.
\label{eq:total-membrane-heat}
\end{equation}

The total conductive heat-transfer rate through all hollow fibers is calculated
as

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{cond}}
=
\left|
N_{\mathrm{fbr}}
\int_{0}^{L}
\frac{
2\pi\overline{k}_m
}{
\ln\left(r_s/r_l\right)
}
\left(
T_{m,s}-T_{m,l}
\right)
\,\mathrm{d}z
\right|.
\label{eq:total-conductive-heat}
\end{equation}

The total latent heat-transfer rate associated with water-vapor transport is

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}
=
\left|
N_{\mathrm{fbr}}
\int_{0}^{L}
2\pi r_l
j_{wl}h_{fg}
\,\mathrm{d}z
\right|.
\label{eq:total-vapor-heat}
\end{equation}

The external heat demand is calculated by subtracting the heat recovered in
HX-1 and HX-2 from the total thermal demand:

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{ext}}
=
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{tot}}
-
\left(
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},1}
+
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},2}
\right).
\label{eq:external-heat-demand}
\end{equation}

The total thermal demand required to heat the feed stream is

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{tot}}
=
\rho_3\dot{V}_3c_{p,3}
\left(
T_{e,s}-T_{e,l}
\right).
\label{eq:total-thermal-demand}
\end{equation}

The heat-transfer rate recovered in HX-1 is

\begin{multline}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},1}
=
\rho_3\dot{V}_3
\left(
c_{p,4}T_4-c_{p,3}T_3
\right)
\\
=
\rho_{11}\dot{V}_{11}
\left(
c_{p,11}T_{11}-c_{p,12}T_{12}
\right).
\label{eq:hx1-recovered-heat}
\end{multline}

The heat-transfer rate recovered in HX-2 is

\begin{multline}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},2}
=
\rho_1\dot{V}_1
\left(
c_{p,2}T_2-c_{p,1}T_1
\right)
\\
=
\rho_6\dot{V}_6
\left(
c_{p,6}T_6-c_{p,7}T_7
\right).
\label{eq:hx2-recovered-heat}
\end{multline}

The global $\mathrm{GOR}$ and $\mathrm{SEC}_{th}$ values are calculated as
average values over the complete simulation period. The volumes of distilled
water produced and brackish well water consumed are obtained by integrating
the corresponding instantaneous volumetric flow rates over time.

A computational code for solving the coupled system of ordinary and partial
differential equations governing the temperatures, flow rates, and salinities
was developed in Fortran~95. The MMOCLH routine \citep{IMSL2022}, which solves
hyperbolic partial differential equations using the method of lines, was
employed with an error tolerance of $10^{-5}$.

The nonlinear equations used to calculate the membrane-interface
temperatures, $T_{m,s}$ and $T_{m,l}$, were solved with a prescribed error
tolerance of $10^{-11}$ using the ZBREN routine \citep{IMSL2022}.

\section{The System Operating Modes}

To control the operation of the dynamic system and optimize the desalination system water
production and brackish water consumption, a general algorithm based on volume and
salinity of the mixture in the mixing reservoir is applied at each instant of time. The
developed computational code is very flexible, allowing simulations of different
desalination system operation scenarios: steady-state regime (no recirculation), batch
recirculation, semi-batch recirculation, continuous recirculation (dynamic rejection
fraction/recirculation), and partial recirculation operation mode with volume or
concentration control.

For the unsteady-state regimes, the system can start the operation with no brine
rejection (total recirculation), $\lambda=\lambda_0=0$, or with a predefined
rejection (partial recirculation), $\lambda_0\neq0$. For these regimes, the
desalination system operates in such a way that the mixing tank, initially with a volume
of brackish water of $V_{mix,0}=100$~L, has its mixture consumed by the DCMD module,
as distillate is produced over time.

Additionally, to analyze the influence of the external heat exchangers on the system
performance it is sufficient to vary their effectiveness ($\varepsilon_{HX1}$ and
$\varepsilon_{HX2}$). By choosing $\varepsilon_{HX1}=0$ and/or $\varepsilon_{HX2}=0$,
the heat exchangers are not included in the analysis.

\subsection{Batch and semi-batch operations}
\label{subsec:batch-semibatch}

In batch and semi-batch operation modes, over each process cycle, the distillate is
continuously removed from the system, and the remaining solution that leaves the MD
module, with higher salinity, is directed to the feed tank ($\lambda=0$). In the batch
operation process, there is no water pumping from the well ($\dot{V}_{well}=0$) between
recycling, so the volume of the solution in the tank decreases, and the salt concentration
increases over time. In the semi-batch operation cycle, the volume of the mixing tank is
kept constant, by continuously adding fresh feed water from the well to the system, at
the same rate at which distillate is removed ($\dot{V}_{well}=\dot{V}_{jwl}$).

In both operation processes, the permeate production rate and the heat-transfer
rate decrease as the feed stream of the MD module becomes increasingly saturated with
salts. After the salinity of the solution in the mixing tank reaches a limiting value,
the high-salinity brine is flushed-out ($\lambda$ is set to 1) until the minimum allowable volume
is reached. Then, the well pump refills the tank from the well ($\dot{V}_{well}=\dot{V}_s$) until the minimum concentration is reached, and the cycle restarts. During these
processes (flushing and filling), the HX-2 should be used to improve the system's
efficiency.

\subsection{Continuous operation (steady-state operation with partial brine recirculation)}
\label{subsec:continuous}

In the continuous operation regime, a fixed operational brine salinity is specified at
the MD module exit. Therefore, after the initial transients have elapsed and the solution
that leaves the module is at the required salinity (reaching the steady-state regime), it
must be continuously withdrawn from the system at a specific fraction rejection ($\lambda$)
that depends on the MD module performance. The make-up water from the well
($\dot{V}_{well}$) is simultaneously added to the tank at a proportional flow rate that
maintains the salinity of the stream at the MD module exit, which is associated with the
corresponding rejection fraction. Again, the HX-2 should be used to improve the system's
efficiency. The rejection fraction and the volumetric flow rate from the well are
obtained from the following salt and total-mass balances applied to the mixing tank:

\begin{equation}
\lambda
=
\frac{
\left(f_{3}-f_{2}\right)\dot{V}_{\mathrm{well}}
+
\left(f_{3}-f_{8}\right)\dot{V}_{7}
}{
\left(f_{2}-f_{8}\right)\dot{V}_{7}
}
\label{eq:l}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\dot{V}_{well}
=
\dot{V}_{jwl}
+
\lambda
\left(\dot{V}_s-\dot{V}_{jwl}\right)
\label{eq:Vwell}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Steady-state operation with no brine recirculation}
\label{subsec:steady-state}

To simulate a steady-state condition with no recirculation, it is sufficient to set
$\lambda=\lambda_0=\lambda_1=1$ and $\dot{V}_{well}=\dot{V}_s$ all the time
during the simulations. In this situation, all retentate leaving the DCMD module is
rejected and the system is continuously fed by the well at constant feed flow rate and
salinity. As will be shown, if this mode of operation is mandatory, as in the case studied 
by \citet{Sampaio2022}, the use of the second heat exchanger (HX-2) to recover heat from 
the retentate stream before it is flushed out leads to an improvement in the performance
of the desalination system.

\subsection{Volume or Concentration controlling}
\label{subsec:vol-conc-control}

Because these operating modes are inherently transient, the distillate is continuously 
withdrawn from the system, while the concentrated stream leaving the MD module, whose 
salinity progressively increases, is either recirculated to the mixing tank or discharged
from the system, depending on the value of $\lambda$. In both cases, the use of HX-2 to recover 
thermal energy from the retentate stream before its discharge improves the overall energy efficiency 
of the desalination system, as demonstrated later.

In the volume-controlled mixing-tank operating mode, predefined minimum and maximum tank volumes are 
established as operational limits. As distillate is produced, the salinity of the solution in the mixing
tank, $c_{s,\mathrm{mix}}(t)$, continuously increases, whereas the liquid volume, $V_{\mathrm{mix}}(t)$, 
decreases. When the minimum allowable volume, $V_{\min}$, is reached, brackish water from the well is 
supplied to the tank until the maximum predefined volume, $V_{\max}$, is restored. This cycle is repeated 
throughout the desalination process. If the solution in the mixing tank reaches a predefined maximum operating 
salinity, the tank contents are completely discharged, corresponding to $\lambda = 1$. The tank is then
refilled with brackish well water until the initial volume, $V_{\mathrm{mix},0}$, is restored.

On the other hand, in the mixing tank salinity controlling operation, a minimum and a maximum
allowed mixing tank salinity is set as the operation limits. The salinity of the water in the
mixing tank ($c_{s,\mathrm{mix}}(t)$) is continuously increasing with the distillate production,
while its volume ($V_{\mathrm{mix}}(t)$) decreases since there is no brackish water flow from
the well. At the instant the maximum salinity allowed is reached ($C_{s,\max}$), the pump from
the well is turned on to feed the mixing tank until the salinity of the water contained in it
falls to the minimum predefined value. In this process, the volume of the mixing tank is
increased, so its operational range will be dynamically adjusted according to the values of
salinity limits previously set.

Figures~\ref{fig:tempsteady}a and \ref{fig:tempsteady}b illustrate bulk temperature
profiles of the feed and permeate channels along the module for different conditions of
volumetric flow rate of the streams, considering steady-state single-pass flow in the

$\varepsilon_{HX1} = \varepsilon_{HX2} = 0$). The objective is to obtain indications of
possible flow configurations (well and distillate flow rates) with adequate potential for
using heat exchangers to enhance the energy efficiency of the desalination system, as
well as to optimize the water recovery (maximize the distillate flux).

Figure~\ref{fig:tempsteady}a illustrates two scenarios where the distillate flow rate is
lower than the feed flow rate, and two scenarios where the distillate flow rate is
greater than the feed flow rate, approximating what \citet{Christie2020} called the DLR
(Distillate Limiting Regime) and the FLR (Feed Limiting Regime) configurations,
respectively. Figure~\ref{fig:tempsteady}b shows three cases where the distillate and
feed flow rates are equal.

At a first glance, observing the streams outlet temperatures of the DLR configuration on
Figure~\ref{fig:tempsteady}a, one could point out that this configuration would have a
great potential for energy recovery, since both streams present the highest outlet
temperatures ($79.6\,^\circ$C for the distillate stream, and $58.8\,^\circ$C for the feed
stream), while in the FLR configuration only the distillate stream presents potential for
heat recovery ($44.8\,^\circ$C). However, as it will be shown, the DLR configuration
presents the lowest water recovery factor and the highest specific thermal energy cost,
becoming inappropriate for heat regeneration.

For an intuitive understanding of how the operational and configurational decisions
affect energy efficiency, \citet{Christie2020}, based on \citet{Lin2014}, introduced
the critical mass-flow-rate ratio, $\alpha^*$, to define the theoretical condition for
the maximum water-recovery factor in steady-state, single-pass DCMD systems,
$R_{\max}$:

\begin{equation}
R_{\max}^{\mathrm{DLR}}
=
\frac{\dot{J}_{wl,\mathrm{sys},\max}}
{\dot{m}_{s}}
=
\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{th}}\,c_{pl}}
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{vap}}}
\,\alpha\,\Delta T
\label{eq:Rmax_DLR}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_{\max}^{\mathrm{FLR}}
=
\frac{\dot{J}_{wl,\mathrm{sys},\max}}
{\dot{m}_{s}}
=
\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{th}}\,c_{ps}}
{\overline{h}_{\mathrm{vap}}}
\,\Delta T
\label{eq:Rmax_FLR}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\alpha
=
\frac{\dot{m}_{l}}
{\dot{m}_{s}}
\label{eq:flow_rate_ratio_alpha}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\alpha^{*}
=
\frac{c_{ps}}{c_{pl}}
\left(
\frac{
\overline{h}_{\mathrm{vap}}
-
\dfrac{c_{ps}\Delta T}{2}
}{
\overline{h}_{\mathrm{vap}}
+
\dfrac{c_{pl}\Delta T}{2}
}
\right)
\label{eq:critical_flow_rate_ratio}
\end{equation}

For $\alpha < \alpha^{*}$, the module operates in the distillate-limited regime (DLR). In this regime, the distillate flow rate is lower than the feed flow rate, and the distillate temperature increases along the module toward the feed-side threshold temperature, $T_{\mathrm{thres},s}$. This threshold is defined as the temperature at which the distillate stream, with $C_s = 0$, has the same partial vapor pressure as the feed stream at the inlet temperature $T_{e,s}$ and salinity $C_s$. Therefore,

\begin{equation}
p_v\left(C_s,T_{e,s}\right)
=
p_v\left(0,T_{\mathrm{thres},s}\right),
\label{eq:threshold_temperature_DLR}
\end{equation}

at which point the transmembrane vapor-pressure difference vanishes.

Conversely, for $\alpha > \alpha^{*}$, the module operates in the feed-limited regime (FLR). In this regime, the feed flow rate is lower than the distillate flow rate, and the feed temperature decreases along the module toward the distillate-side threshold temperature, $T_{\mathrm{thres},l}$. This threshold is defined as the temperature at which the feed stream, with salinity $C_s$, has the same partial vapor pressure as the distillate stream, with $C_s = 0$, at the distillate inlet temperature $T_{e,l}$. Thus,

\begin{equation}
p_v\left(C_s,T_{\mathrm{thres},l}\right)
=
p_v\left(0,T_{e,l}\right).
\label{eq:threshold_temperature_FLR}
\end{equation}

When the local temperature difference remains greater than the corresponding threshold 
temperature difference over the entire MD module, vapor transport occurs throughout the
whole membrane length. Under these conditions, the process may become limited only by 
mass transfer, characterizing the mass-transfer-limited regime (MTLR), which may result
from insufficient membrane area or a very low membrane mass-transfer coefficient. 
Therefore, provided that mass-transfer limitations are negligible, a DCMD module 
operates under one of the two thermodynamically limited regimes: DLR or FLR.

According to \citet{Christie2020}, for single-pass counter-current DCMD desalination,
the FLR configuration is the most suitable operating condition for maximizing water
recovery. Only under FLR conditions does the distillate stream have sufficient heat-capacity
flow rate to sustain the maximum attainable evaporation of the feed stream. Consequently,

\begin{equation}
R_{\max}^{\mathrm{FLR}}
>
R_{\max}^{\mathrm{DLR}}.
\label{eq:Rmax_FLR_greater_DLR}
\end{equation}

Table~\ref{tab:table6} presents the average theoretical maximum water-recovery ratio, 
distillate mass flux, temperature-polarization coefficient, and thermal-performance 
indicators of the DCMD module, including the gain output ratio (GOR) and the specific 
thermal energy consumption, $\mathrm{SEC}_{\mathrm{th}}$. Three scenarios involving the
external heat exchangers were investigated for two feed-salinity levels and different
feed and distillate flow rates: operation without heat recovery, with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HX},1} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{HX},2} = 0$; 
operation with HX-1 only, with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HX},1} = 0.8$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HX},2} = 0$;
and operation with both HX-1 and HX-2, with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HX},1} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{HX},2} = 0.8$.

The results presented in Table~\ref{tab:table6} are consistent with the conclusions of \citet{Christie2020}, 
indicating that the most favorable operating conditions correspond to FLR configurations in which $\alpha \approx \alpha^{*}$ 
and no threshold temperature is reached within the module. Under these conditions, high water-recovery ratios, $RR$, and the 
lowest values of $\mathrm{SEC}_{\mathrm{th}}$ are obtained.

Under DLR conditions, for which $\alpha < \alpha^{*}$, decreasing the flow-rate ratio $\alpha$ reduces the water-recovery
ratio and increases the specific thermal energy consumption. This behavior makes the configuration unattractive,
even when the second heat exchanger, HX-2, is used to recover part of the thermal energy from the retentate stream. 
Conversely, under FLR conditions with $\alpha \gg \alpha^{*}$, the highest water-recovery ratios are achieved, 
although at the expense of substantially higher energy consumption. Moreover, under these conditions, the inclusion
of HX-2 produces no significant improvement in the overall performance of the DCMD system.

Table~\ref{tab:table6} also shows that, as the salinity of the brackish feed water increases, operating with a 
flow-rate ratio slightly greater than the critical value, $\alpha^{*}$, can improve the water-recovery ratio,
$RR$, the distillate volumetric flow rate, $\dot{V}_{\mathrm{jwl}}$, and the thermal efficiency, $\eta_{\mathrm{th}}$, 
while causing only a small increase in $\mathrm{SEC}_{\mathrm{th}}$.

The cycle duration of each transient operating mode can be identified by the
sharp decrease in the mixing-tank volume shown in Figure~\ref{fig:modes}(c),
which occurs when the solution is discharged from the tank, or by the abrupt
increase in the rejection fraction shown in Figure~\ref{fig:modes}(e). These
events are accompanied by corresponding variations in the mixture temperature,
Figure~\ref{fig:modes}(d), and in the brackish-water flow rate,
Figure~\ref{fig:modes}(f). The cycle duration is approximately 1789~s
($\approx30$~min) for batch operation, 4800~s
($\approx1$~h~20~min) for V-Control operation, and 6090~s
($\approx1$~h~41~min) for semi-batch operation.

Although the system is emptied and subsequently refilled with fresh brackish
water from the well at $T_{\mathrm{well}}=25~^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the temperature
of the mixture in the tank does not return to this value during each cycle
because of the thermal inertia of the system. In the procedure adopted here,
emptying and refilling occur sequentially: the tank is continuously emptied
until a predefined minimum volume is reached and is then continuously refilled
to the initial volume, $V_{\mathrm{mix},0}=100~\mathrm{L}$.

To identify the most appropriate operating mode from the perspectives of energy
efficiency and water recovery, Figures~\ref{fig:sec} and~\ref{fig:rr} compare
the average specific thermal energy consumption,
$\overline{\mathrm{SEC}}_{\mathrm{th}}$, and the water recovery ratio,
$\mathrm{RR}$, respectively.

The black bars with gradient and solid fills represent the scenarios with
maximum salt concentrations of
$C_{s,\max}=120~\mathrm{g_s\,L_{mix}^{-1}}$ and
$C_{s,\max}=260~\mathrm{g_s\,L_{mix}^{-1}}$, respectively. Thus, under the
transient batch, V-Control, and semi-batch modes, the salinity in the mixing tank
varies from the brackish-water well salinity to the specified value of
$C_{s,\max}$. In the steady-state modes, operation at a prescribed value of
$C_{s,\max}$ requires the final brine or the brackish-water/brine stream supplied
to the system to have the same salinity; consequently,
$C_{s,\mathrm{oper}}=C_{s,\max}$.

Overall, these results show that a balance must be sought between specific
thermal energy consumption and water recovery because, for a given maximum
operating salinity, a trade-off exists between these metrics. In other words,
low specific thermal energy consumption and high water recovery cannot
generally be achieved simultaneously.

For the same limiting mixture salinity, batch operation provides the lowest
energy consumption, followed by the semi-batch, steady-state, and continuous
modes, in agreement with the literature
\citep{SwaminathanLienhard2018}. Regarding water recovery,
Figure~\ref{fig:rr} shows that the semi-batch mode provides the highest recovery
ratio, followed by the V-Control and batch modes. Steady-state operation is
unfavorable from this perspective unless the system is of the type studied by
\citet{Sampaio2022}, for which complete rejection of the retentate leaving the
desalination module is acceptable.

The performance of the V-Control mode at an intermediate salt concentration is
particularly noteworthy. For
$C_{s,\max}=120~\mathrm{g_s\,L_{mix}^{-1}}$, this mode produces slightly more
distillate per unit volume of brackish well water than the semi-batch mode,
with recovery values of
$76.4~\mathrm{L_{dist}\,L_{well}^{-1}}$ and
$76.2~\mathrm{L_{dist}\,L_{well}^{-1}}$, respectively, at the same average
specific thermal energy consumption,
$\overline{\mathrm{SEC}}_{\mathrm{th}}=0.40~\mathrm{kWh\,L^{-1}}$.
However, this difference may be negligible because the energy requirements
associated with process control and intermittent pumping were not included in
the present analysis.

Figure~\ref{fig:rr} also shows that operating the desalination unit continuously
at a salinity equal to the average salinity of the batch mode, represented by
the blue bars, can result in lower energy consumption than batch operation, but
at the expense of a lower water recovery ratio.

Therefore, for implementation in regions subject to seasonal water stress, such
as the semi-arid region of northeastern Brazil, the results indicate that
semi-batch operation may be the preferred alternative for brackish-water
desalination when the primary objective is to minimize the consumption of
brackish well water. Nevertheless, the energy costs associated with pumps,
control devices, and other accessories required by each operating mode should
be included in a broader thermoeconomic analysis.

\section{Conclusions}

The present work developed a transient physical-mathematical model and its
computational implementation for a hollow-fiber DCMD desalination plant operating
with dual heat recovery from the distillate and retentate streams and with total
or partial recirculation of the concentrated stream. Six operating modes were
examined under conditions representative of brackish-water wells in the semi-arid
Brazilian northeastern region, allowing energy efficiency and water conservation
to be assessed together rather than in isolation.

Beyond confirming that heat recovery from the distillate is a mandatory feature
of any competitive DCMD plant, the results reposition the retentate stream,
which conventional designs discard together with the concentrated brine, as a
thermal resource whose recovery through a second heat exchanger substantially
alters the standing of DCMD in the desalination landscape. The magnitude of the
efficiency gain enabled by HX-2 is large enough to narrow the historical gap
between thermal separation and reverse osmosis in the small-to-medium capacity
range ($<100$~m$^3$/day), which is precisely the range in which RO loses
competitiveness because of high specific capital cost and sensitivity to feed
variability. This is a qualitative shift more than a numerical one: it indicates
that DCMD, when equipped with dual heat recovery, is not merely a niche
technology for waste-heat valorization but a viable alternative wherever
low-grade thermal energy (solar collectors, industrial residual heat, engine
exhaust) is locally available and RO scaling is uneconomical.

A second consequence of the transient analysis concerns the role of the critical
mass-flow-rate ratio, $\alpha^{*}$, as an \emph{operational} rather than merely
\emph{design} parameter. Because the temperature and salinity of the well feed
vary seasonally in hydrologically stressed regions, and because $\alpha^{*}$
depends on both, fixed-flow DCMD operation is inherently suboptimal for such
applications: the proximity to $\alpha^{*}$ determines not only the
water-recovery ratio but also whether HX-2 delivers a meaningful benefit at all.
Under FLR conditions with $\alpha \gg \alpha^{*}$, the retentate leaves the
module already close to ambient temperature and little sensible heat remains to
be recovered, so the second heat exchanger becomes essentially inert. This has
direct implications for control-system design: adaptive strategies that track
$\alpha^{*}$ as the feed drifts would extract considerably more value from the
dual-recovery architecture than the fixed operating points typically reported in
the literature.

The comparison across operating modes reveals a trade-off that cannot be
resolved by pure thermodynamic optimization. Batch operation minimizes specific
thermal energy consumption because the tank's thermal inertia keeps the average
feed salinity, and thus the transmembrane vapor-pressure driving force,
favorable during most of each cycle, but it periodically discharges brackish
water that has not been fully exploited. Semi-batch operation eliminates this
waste and achieves recovery ratios above ninety percent, at the cost of a
slightly higher energy demand, because fresh well water is supplied at the same
rate at which distillate is produced and no volume is lost during flush-and-refill
transients. For the semi-arid Brazilian context that motivated this study, and
for analogous regions in which the shadow price of well water exceeds the shadow
price of thermal energy, a common situation whenever solar collectors or
industrial waste heat are locally available, semi-batch operation is
therefore the physically appropriate choice, not the mode that a purely
energy-based figure of merit would select. The volume-controlled mode emerges
as a practical middle ground that retains most of the semi-batch water recovery
while preserving batch-level energy efficiency, and deserves attention in future
pilot deployments.

More broadly, the transient framework developed here calls into question the
widespread practice of characterizing MD desalination plants through
steady-state metrics alone. The overshoots in $\mathrm{GOR}$ and undershoots in
$\mathrm{SEC}_{\mathrm{th}}$ observed during each flush-and-refill are not
artifacts to be averaged away; they are the physical mechanism through which
HX-2 delivers most of its benefit, because it is precisely during those
transients that hot retentate flows past HX-2 while cold well water is being
drawn in. Any economic assessment or control-loop tuning built on steady-state
assumptions will therefore systematically underestimate the value of dual heat
recovery and, by extension, the competitiveness of DCMD for the small-scale
applications discussed above.

Two lines of extension follow naturally. The first is the incorporation of the
pumping, control, and instrumentation costs that were deliberately excluded
from the present energy-efficiency analysis, so that a full thermoeconomic
ranking of the six operating modes can be produced, a step required before
any recommendation on operating mode can be turned into a design specification
for a real plant. The second is the extension of the modeling framework to AGMD
and V-AGMD configurations, which offer higher intrinsic thermal efficiencies
but were not addressed here, in order to simulate the entire TRL-6 experimental
desalination unit installed at LabMEMS/UFRJ, which is controlled through the
volume of the collector tank. Together, these developments should support the
transition of hollow-fiber MD from a laboratory technique toward field
deployment in the specific regional context that motivated this study.
