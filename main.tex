\begin{abstract}
A transient model for hollow-fiber direct contact membrane distillation (DCMD) modules
is employed to evaluate a desalination plant in six modes of operation, namely batch,
semi-batch, volume-control, salinity-control, continuous, and steady state. The main
goal is to reveal DCMD desalination configurations that present the best compromise
between maximum energy efficiency and minimum use of brackish water from wells, mainly
focusing on applications in sites under hydrological stress, like in the semi-arid
Brazilian northeastern region. The obtained results indicate that using an additional
heat exchanger for heat regeneration, combined with recirculation of the retentate
stream, enhances the system efficiency compared to a scenario that uses only the
distillate heat recovery. Additionally, the results also showed that if the main
interest is in obtaining high recovery ratios (high volume of produced distillate water
per volume of brackish water), the recirculating semi-batch and volume-controlling
operating modes are the best alternatives to produce distillated water. However, if
the interest is to minimize the thermal energy cost, the best option is the batch
operating mode.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Desalination; DCMD system; heat recovery; retentate recirculation;
energy efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Freshwater scarcity, mainly due to population growth, has attracted the attention of
researchers and industry for the development and improvement of techniques for the
treatment, purification, and desalination of salt water. Among the main desalination
techniques, those involving thermal separation methods, such as membrane distillation,
require more energy due to the need to vaporize the water since the water vaporization
enthalpy is about 2{,}400~kJ/kg \citep{Sharqawy2010}, while Gibbs free energy for
seawater, which governs the energy use of the reverse osmosis method, is 2.7~kJ/kg
\citep{Elimelech2011}. Therefore, one of the challenges that must be overcome for the
application of thermal methods such as membrane distillation (MD) in water desalination,
which aims to be competitive against the conventionally employed reverse osmosis (RO)
process in small and medium-sized systems ($<100$~m$^3$/day of water produced), is to
minimize the demand for thermal energy, improving thermal efficiency.

The membrane distillation process enables the use of residual heat from other thermal
processes or the heat available from renewable sources, such as solar collectors.
However, heat losses by conduction through the membrane further increase the thermal
consumption, which, when added to high-water consumption, limits the large-scale use of
these systems \citep{Khayet2011}. Nevertheless, according to \citet{Guan2015}, when
compared to conventional desalination processes, such as multi-stage flash distillation,
multi-effect distillation, and reverse osmosis, the MD process has several benefits:
low salinity sensitivity and high salt rejection (100\% content, theoretically), low
membrane vulnerability to fouling, greater performance under low severity operating
conditions, and the viability of using low-grade waste heat from other thermal processes
or renewable sources, such as solar collectors, as previously mentioned
\citep{Curcio2005, Charcosset2009, Drioli2015}.

Concerning its technology, according to the method of obtaining the vapor pressure
gradient through the membrane, the MD is classified in the following configurations
\citep{Zhang2015, Swaminathan2018}: DCMD (Direct Contact Membrane Distillation), AGMD
(Air Gap Membrane Distillation), SGMD (Sweeping Gas Membrane Distillation), VMD
(Vacuum Membrane Distillation), PGMD (Permeate Gap Membrane Distillation), CGMD
(Conductive Gap Membrane Distillation), V-AGMD (Vacuum-Enhanced Air Gap Membrane
Distillation), among other variants. Each configuration has its advantages and intrinsic
disadvantages, so the choice of one of them is still a research theme, which is usually
directed through metrics such as GOR (Gained Output Ratio) and SEC (Specific Energy
Consumption), which are parameters for energy efficiency analysis. The DCMD is the most
studied and straightforward configuration among them, widely used for desalination
studies and concentration of aqueous solutions.

In general, the increase in the thermal efficiency of MD systems is achieved by improving
the performance of membranes (by choosing membranes with low thermal conductivity,
adequate porosity, pore size and pore size distribution, and thickness), as well as by
reducing the amount of external heat to be supplied to the process
\citep{Gostoli1998, Khayet2006, AlObaidani2008, Yao2013, Li2014, Swaminathan2018}.

The heat supplied to the process can then be significantly reduced by the reuse of the
heat available in the output currents of the desalination module through external heat
exchangers, extending the applicability of the MD process and improving energy efficiency
\citep{Gilron2007, Lee2011, Hausmann2012, Lu2012, Zhang2015, GonzalezBravo2015,
Kim2015, Maheswari2015, Lin2014, Gustafson2018, Christie2020, Lisboa2021, Sampaio2022}.
Obviously, operating conditions such as temperature, the feed and distillate flow rates,
salinity of the feed stream, and its recirculation, among others, significantly affect
the MD process and can improve or reduce process efficiency
\citep{Khayet2000, Wang2009, Zhang2010, Fan2012, Alkhudhiri2012, Guan2014}.

Additionally, since MD presents a low recovery ratio per pass (ratio between the
production rate of pure water and the feed flow), using some form of recirculation of
the brine/retentate stream is mandatory to increase efficiency. In this way, by
considering a CGMD module, \citet{SwaminathanLienhard2018} describe four operation
strategies that enable high global recovery using a single-pass low recovery process
(batch recirculation, semi-batch recirculation, continuous recirculation, and continuous
multi-stage recirculation). The first two operations are classified as
transient/discontinuous processes, while the last two are steady state ones. According
to these authors, who studied the salinity variation from a lower limit of 70~g/kg to an
upper limit of 260~g/kg, the batch operation is the best option since GOR is about twice
for the same distillate flow when compared to the continuous recirculation operation.

\citet{Sampaio2022} modeled a seawater desalination DCMD plant with heat recovery of
the recirculating hot distillate stream by using a DCMD module composed by a bundle of
hollow-fiber tubes. He combined analytical solutions for the mass transfer problem
across the membranes with the finite volume method for solution the steady-state heat
transfer problem. Still, based on \citet{Christie2020} and \citet{Lin2014}, and to
extend their works, \citet{Sampaio2022} developed an approximation for the optimal
value of the ratio between permeate and feed flow rates, and for the maximum mass
recovery rate that, differently from the expressions developed by \citet{Christie2020}
and \citet{Lin2014}, take into consideration the membrane thermal efficiency.

Thus, based on the works of \citet{Swaminathan2018}, \citet{Christie2020},
\citet{Lisboa2021}, and \citet{Sampaio2022} and extending their analyses, the present
work aims at the development of physical-mathematical modeling, in the unsteady-state
regime, and subsequent numerical simulation of a hollow-fiber DCMD system with double
recirculation and heat recovery. The main goal is firstly to evaluate the potential of
heat recovery from the retentate stream to increase the energy efficiency of the system
to produce distilled water. Furthermore, an application of the model to study the
desalination process in regions with high seasonal hydrological stress, such as the
semi-arid region of the Brazilian northeastern. In addition to the classical batch,
semi-batch, continuous, and steady-state modes of operation, two intermediary modes,
namely, mixing tank volume controlling and mixing tank salt concentration controlling,
are also introduced. The aim is to verify their effect on the behavior of the system
efficiency and on the water recovery ratio (for minimizing the well brackish water
consumption). These controlling schemes can also be operated in conjunction with total or
partial recirculation, i.e., when the brine is rejected from the system with fraction
rejections equal or different to the unity, respectively.

\section{System Description}

The MD desalination plant modelled in the present work is characterized by a water
desalination system that operates in the transient regime, containing a DCMD module (a
vessel with a bundle of hollow fiber membranes), two tanks (a mixing and collector), and
running with dual recirculation and dual heat recovery, where the heat available at the
output of the module from the feed and distillate streams are recovered through the heat
exchangers, HX-1 and HX-2, respectively, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:system}. The heat
source and sink are constant-temperature heat reservoirs, so the feed and distillate
streams reach the specified values of the source/sink temperatures by supplying/extracting
exactly the corresponding amount of heat required.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{IMAGENS/fig1_system.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the DCMD system with double heat recovery and partial or total recirculation of the retentate stream.}
    \label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

At the initial time, the feed circuit is completely filled with brackish water at the
well salinity ($c_s^{well}$) and ambient temperature ($T_{amb}$). The distillate circuit
is filled with distilled water at the same ambient temperature. The feed stream leaves
the mixing tank at state~3 ($c_{s3}$, $V_3 = V_{feed}$, $T_3$) and enters HX-1, which
heats it to state~4 ($c_{s4}$, $V_4$, $T_4$) by receiving energy from the distillate
leaving the DCMD module at state~11 ($c_{s11}$, $V_{11}$, $T_{11}$). The mandatory use
of this heat exchanger (HX-1) greatly increases the efficiency of the MD system by
reducing the amount of heat ($q_h$) that must be supplied by the heater to raise the feed
stream temperature to the working temperature at state~5 ($c_{s5}$, $V_5$, $T_5 =
T_{feed}$).

The concentrated module effluent (the retentate) at state~6 ($c_{s6}$, $V_6$, $T_6$)
can be removed from the system (as a reject stream) or it can be recycled (recirculated).
In the present work, the computational code was built taking into consideration both
possibilities, simultaneously or not, depending on a flow rejection fraction ($\lambda$)
at state~9 ($c_{s9}$, $V_9$, $T_9$), which directly and dynamically impacts the
concentration of the solution in the tank and its volume, $V_{mix}$. When $\lambda = 1$,
the brine is completely rejected at state~9 (the MD system must be continuously fed from
the well on a permanent basis). Otherwise, when $\lambda < 1$, the retentate is partially
recycled/rejected.

When necessary, depending on salinity and volume of the mixing tank (or the operating
regime), brackish water can be pumped from the well at state~1 ($V_{well}$, $c_s^{well}$,
$T_{well}$), passing through HX-2 as a cold stream to gain energy carried by the effluent
retentate from the DCMD module at state~6, which will be cooled down to state~7 ($c_{s7}$,
$Q_7$, $T_7$). The stream at state~7 can be directed, either totally ($\lambda = 0$) or
partially ($0 < \lambda < 1$), to the mixing tank through state~8 ($c_{s8}$, $V_8$, $T_8$),
to be mixed with the heated brackish water stream coming from state~2.

Thus, in addition to the desalination-system operating modes---batch,
semi-batch, volume- or salinity-controlled, continuous, and steady-state
operation---the numerical simulation will be used to investigate the energy
efficiency of the system when an additional heat exchanger, HX-2, is employed.
This heat exchanger enables thermal exchange between the retentate stream and
the brackish water supplied by the well. The simulation will also evaluate the
partial rejection or recirculation of the retentate stream after heat recovery.

To extend the scope of the simulation, a parametric analysis will be performed
to evaluate the interaction between the NaCl concentration in the feed stream
and the amount of heat recovered from the fluid returning through the feed
circuit of the desalination module. This analysis will also support the
development of operational control strategies aimed at maximizing desalination
efficiency while reducing brackish-water consumption from the well.To perform the present study, 
the operational ranges of the PLICA-7 DCMD module produced by Aquastill for experiments in a relevant 
environment are adopted.

\section{Mathematical Model}

The implemented mathematical model incorporates heat and mass transfer in all system
components: mixing tank, distillate tank, DCMD module, heat source and sink, and heat
exchangers 1 and 2. For the DCMD module, the model proposed by \citet{Lisboa2021} was
extended to the unsteady-state regime. The authors treated the hollow-fiber DCMD module
as an equivalent porous medium composed of the following constituents: the porous shell
medium (where the feed stream flows), the porous lumen medium (where the distillate
stream flows), and the membrane (where the distillation process occurs).An illustrative 
figure of the porous media model employed to analyze the heat and mass transfer in the vessel 
is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:porousmedia}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{IMAGENS/2.jpg}
  \caption{Illustration of the porous media models. Cross sections of the: (a)~Porous
  medium, lumen side; (b)~Module and hollow-fiber membrane bundle; (c)~Porous medium,
  shell side. Figure~1 from \citet{Lisboa2021}. Reprinted with permission from Elsevier.
  Copyright 2021.}
  \label{fig:porousmedia}
\end{figure}

The hollow-fiber membranes are assumed to be uniformly distributed over the vessel
(module) cross-section, with their axes parallel to the vessel axis. The module porosity
is defined as the ratio of the phase volume to the total vessel volume:

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
=
m
+
(-1)^{m}
N_{\mathrm{fbr}}
\frac{r_{\gamma}^{2}}{r_{v}^{2}},
\label{eq:epsilon-gamma}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
m
=
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } \gamma=l,\\
1, & \text{if } \gamma=s.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:m-gamma}
\end{equation}


Here, $\varepsilon_\gamma$ is the porosity of phase $\gamma$ (with $\gamma=l$ for the
lumen phase and $\gamma=s$ for the shell phase), $N_{fbr}$ is the number of fibers in
the vessel, $r_\gamma$ is the radius of the $\gamma$ phase, and $r_v$ is the radius of
the vessel. Given that the membrane has a thickness $\delta$, the outer radius of the
membrane will be $r_s=r_l+\delta$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{IMAGENS/3.png}
  \caption{Representation for the single-fiber model of the hollow-fiber membrane module.}
  \label{fig:fiber}
\end{figure}

The specific surface area, $a_\gamma$, used in heat and mass transfer calculations, is
the ratio of the surface area available for transport phenomena in each phase to the
vessel volume:

\begin{equation}
a_\gamma
=
\frac{N_{fbr}\,2\pi r_\gamma}{\pi r_v^2}
\label{eq:specsurf}
\end{equation}

Considering that the heat exchangers, the distillate and mixing tanks, and the DCMD
module are adiabatic, and that the salinity and temperature fields inside them are
uniform (except inside the DCMD module), the physical situation is mathematically
modelled as follows.

\subsection{Modeling Mass Transfer in the Membrane}
\label{subsec:membrane-mass-transfer}

To partially close the system of equations, the mass-transfer process inside
the DCMD module must be modeled to determine the water-vapor mass flux through
the membrane, $j_{wl}$. Following the procedure adopted by
\citet{Lisboa2021}, the Dusty Gas Model proposed by
\citet{LawsonLloyd1997}, one of the most widely used models for predicting
distilled-water transport through porous membranes, is employed. The molar transport of water 
vapor through the membrane pores is described by

\begin{equation}
\frac{N_w}{D_w^k}
+
\frac{
p_aN_w-p_wN_a
}{
D_{wa}^{0}
}
=
-\frac{1}{RT_m}
\nabla p_w,
\label{eq:dusty-gas-model}
\end{equation}

where $N_w$ is the molar flux of water vapor, $N_a$ is the molar flux of air,
$p_a$ is the partial pressure of air inside the membrane pores, $p_w$ is the
partial pressure of water vapor, $D_w^k$ is the Knudsen diffusivity of water
vapor, $D_{wa}^{0}$ is the molecular diffusivity of water vapor in air, $R$ is
the universal gas constant, and $T_m$ is the average thermodynamic temperature
at each axial position along the membrane. Air is assumed to remain stationary 
inside the membrane pores, such that

\begin{equation}
N_a
\approx
0.
\label{eq:stationary-air}
\end{equation}

Applying Dalton's law, the molar flux of water vapor through the hollow-fiber
membrane, based on the inner membrane radius, is given by

\begin{equation}
N_{wl}
=
\frac{
D_{wa}^{0}
}{
RT_mr_l\ln\left(r_s/r_l\right)
}
\ln
\left[
\frac{
D_{wa}^{0}-D_{\mathrm{eff}}p_s
}{
D_{wa}^{0}-D_{\mathrm{eff}}p_l
}
\right].
\label{eq:water-molar-flux}
\end{equation}

The corresponding water-vapor mass flux is

\begin{equation}
j_{wl}
=
M_wN_{wl},
\label{eq:water-mass-flux}
\end{equation}

where $M_w$ is the molar mass of water.

The molecular diffusivity of water vapor in air is calculated from

\begin{equation}
D_{wa}^{0}
=
4.46\times10^{-6}
\frac{\varepsilon_m}{\tau_m}
T_m^{2.334}.
\label{eq:water-air-diffusivity}
\end{equation}

The Knudsen diffusivity of water vapor is given by

\begin{equation}
D_w^k
=
\frac{\varepsilon_m}{\tau_m}
\frac{d_p}{3}
\sqrt{
\frac{8RT_m}{\pi M_w}
}.
\label{eq:knudsen-diffusivity}
\end{equation}

The effective diffusivity resulting from the combined molecular and Knudsen
transport mechanisms is

\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{eff}}
=
\frac{
D_w^kD_{wa}^{0}
}{
D_{wa}^{0}+PD_w^k
}.
\label{eq:effective-diffusivity}
\end{equation}

The total pressure inside the membrane pores, $P$, is assumed to be equal to
atmospheric pressure. The water-vapor partial pressures at the shell-side and
lumen-side membrane interfaces, $p_s$ and $p_l$, respectively, are calculated
from the corresponding saturation vapor pressures. The effect of salinity is
included through the salt mole fraction, $x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}$, and the
water activity coefficient, $a_\gamma$, according to

\begin{equation}
p_\gamma
=
\left(
1-x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}
\right)
a_\gamma p_{v,\gamma},
\qquad
\gamma\in\{s,l\},
\label{eq:interfacial-vapor-pressure}
\end{equation}

where $p_{v,\gamma}$ is the saturation vapor pressure evaluated at the
corresponding membrane-interface temperature using the Antoine equation. The activity 
coefficient is calculated as

\begin{equation}
a_\gamma
=
1
-
0.5x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}
-
10x_{\mathrm{salt},\gamma}^{2}.
\label{eq:water-activity-coefficient}
\end{equation}

The morphological characteristics of the membrane are included in the
calculation of the distillate mass flux through the mean pore diameter, $d_p$,
porosity, $\varepsilon_m$, and tortuosity, $\tau_m$.

Models for the effective membrane thermal conductivity,
$\overline{k}_m$, and the membrane tortuosity, $\tau_m$, must also be selected
to close the governing equations. The most common effective-thermal-conductivity
models are based on different geometrical arrangements of the solid and gaseous
phases. In the parallel model, the solid and gaseous phases are arranged in
parallel:

\begin{equation}
\overline{k}_m
=
\left(
1-\varepsilon_m
\right)
\overline{k}_s
+
\varepsilon_m\overline{k}_f,
\qquad
\text{parallel model}.
\label{eq:parallel-conductivity}
\end{equation}

In the series model, the two phases are arranged in series:

\begin{equation}
\overline{k}_m
=
\left[
\frac{
1-\varepsilon_m
}{
\overline{k}_s
}
+
\frac{
\varepsilon_m
}{
\overline{k}_f
}
\right]^{-1},
\qquad
\text{series model}.
\label{eq:series-conductivity}
\end{equation}

For porous media with porosity greater than approximately 0.6,
\citet{GarciaPayoIzquierdoGil2004} recommend the Maxwell I model. This model
assumes that the polymeric matrix consists of a random distribution of solid
particles that do not interact with one another within a homogeneous fluid
medium. The resulting effective conductivity is

\begin{equation}
\overline{k}_m
=
\overline{k}_f
\left[
\frac{
1+2\left(1-\varepsilon_m\right)b_{sf}
}{
1-\left(1-\varepsilon_m\right)b_{sf}
}
\right],
\qquad
\text{Maxwell I model},
\label{eq:maxwell-conductivity}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
b_{sf}
=
\frac{
\overline{k}_s-\overline{k}_f
}{
\overline{k}_s+2\overline{k}_f
}.
\label{eq:maxwell-conductivity-ratio}
\end{equation}

The manner in which the pores and solid matrix are distributed within the
membrane governs the diffusion path and, consequently, the vapor flux.
Membrane tortuosity, defined as the ratio between the actual diffusion path
length and the membrane thickness, is therefore an important parameter for
predicting the transmembrane mass flux.

Several tortuosity models are available in the literature
\citep{Ghanbarian2013}. \citet{Kim2021} analyzed eleven tortuosity models,
including eight geometrical models based on Euclidean structures, such as
cubes, spheres, and cylinders, and three models based on the fractal theory of
porous media. For the geometrical model proposed by \citet{MackieMeares1955}, the membrane
tortuosity is

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{
\left(
2-\varepsilon_m
\right)^2
}{
\varepsilon_m
}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-mackie-meares}
\end{equation}

The model proposed by \citet{Beeckman1990} is

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{
\varepsilon_m
}{
1-
\left(
1-\varepsilon_m
\right)^{1/3}
}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-beeckman}
\end{equation}

For the fractal model proposed by \citet{EliasKohav1991}, the tortuosity is

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{1}{\varepsilon_m}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-elias-kohav}
\end{equation}

The Bruggeman model is given by

\begin{equation}
\tau_m
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_m}}.
\label{eq:tortuosity-bruggeman}
\end{equation}

According to \citep{Curcino2022}, who evaluated several effective-thermal-
conductivity and tortuosity models for membrane-distillation simulations in
flat-sheet and hollow-fiber configurations, an appropriate model should be
selected based on the known morphological and structural properties of the
membrane employed in the experimental desalination module.


\subsection{Efficiency Metrics}
\label{subsec:efficiency-metrics}

To evaluate the desalination system and quantify improvements in the energy
efficiency of the membrane-distillation process, the thermal efficiency,
$\eta_{th}$, gain-output ratio, $\mathrm{GOR}$, specific thermal energy
consumption, $\mathrm{SEC}_{th}$, and water recovery ratio, $\mathrm{RR}$, are
calculated under the different operating scenarios. The thermal efficiency of 
the membrane is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\eta_{th}
=
\frac{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}
}{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{memb}}
}.
\label{eq:thermal-efficiency}
\end{equation}

The gain-output ratio is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{GOR}
=
\frac{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}
}{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{ext}}
}.
\label{eq:gor}
\end{equation}

The specific thermal energy consumption is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SEC}_{th}
=
\frac{
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{ext}}
}{
\dot{V}_{jwl}
}.
\label{eq:sec-thermal}
\end{equation}

The water recovery ratio is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RR}
=
\frac{
\dot{m}_{\mathrm{dist}}
}{
\dot{m}_{\mathrm{well}}
}.
\label{eq:water-recovery-ratio}
\end{equation}

The total heat-transfer rate through the membrane is the sum of the conductive
and latent heat-transfer rates:

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{memb}}
=
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{cond}}
+
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}.
\label{eq:total-membrane-heat}
\end{equation}

The total conductive heat-transfer rate through all hollow fibers is calculated
as

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{cond}}
=
\left|
N_{\mathrm{fbr}}
\int_{0}^{L}
\frac{
2\pi\overline{k}_m
}{
\ln\left(r_s/r_l\right)
}
\left(
T_{m,s}-T_{m,l}
\right)
\,\mathrm{d}z
\right|.
\label{eq:total-conductive-heat}
\end{equation}

The total latent heat-transfer rate associated with water-vapor transport is

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{vap}}
=
\left|
N_{\mathrm{fbr}}
\int_{0}^{L}
2\pi r_l
j_{wl}h_{fg}
\,\mathrm{d}z
\right|.
\label{eq:total-vapor-heat}
\end{equation}

The external heat demand is calculated by subtracting the heat recovered in
HX-1 and HX-2 from the total thermal demand:

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{ext}}
=
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{tot}}
-
\left(
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},1}
+
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},2}
\right).
\label{eq:external-heat-demand}
\end{equation}

The total thermal demand required to heat the feed stream is

\begin{equation}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{tot}}
=
\rho_3\dot{V}_3c_{p,3}
\left(
T_{e,s}-T_{e,l}
\right).
\label{eq:total-thermal-demand}
\end{equation}

The heat-transfer rate recovered in HX-1 is

\begin{multline}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},1}
=
\rho_3\dot{V}_3
\left(
c_{p,4}T_4-c_{p,3}T_3
\right)
\\
=
\rho_{11}\dot{V}_{11}
\left(
c_{p,11}T_{11}-c_{p,12}T_{12}
\right).
\label{eq:hx1-recovered-heat}
\end{multline}

The heat-transfer rate recovered in HX-2 is

\begin{multline}
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{HX},2}
=
\rho_1\dot{V}_1
\left(
c_{p,2}T_2-c_{p,1}T_1
\right)
\\
=
\rho_6\dot{V}_6
\left(
c_{p,6}T_6-c_{p,7}T_7
\right).
\label{eq:hx2-recovered-heat}
\end{multline}

The global $\mathrm{GOR}$ and $\mathrm{SEC}_{th}$ values are calculated as
average values over the complete simulation period. The volumes of distilled
water produced and brackish well water consumed are obtained by integrating
the corresponding instantaneous volumetric flow rates over time.

A computational code for solving the coupled system of ordinary and partial
differential equations governing the temperatures, flow rates, and salinities
was developed in Fortran~95. The MMOCLH routine \citep{IMSL2022}, which solves
hyperbolic partial differential equations using the method of lines, was
employed with an error tolerance of $10^{-5}$.

The nonlinear equations used to calculate the membrane-interface
temperatures, $T_{m,s}$ and $T_{m,l}$, were solved with a prescribed error
tolerance of $10^{-11}$ using the ZBREN routine \citep{IMSL2022}.

\section{The System Operating Modes}

To control the operation of the dynamic system and optimize the desalination system water
production and brackish water consumption, a general algorithm based on volume and
salinity of the mixture in the mixing reservoir is applied at each instant of time. The
developed computational code is very flexible, allowing simulations of different
desalination system operation scenarios: steady-state regime (no recirculation), batch
recirculation, semi-batch recirculation, continuous recirculation (dynamic rejection
fraction/recirculation), and partial recirculation operation mode with volume or
concentration control.

For the unsteady-state regimes, the system can start the operation with no brine
rejection (total recirculation), $\lambda=\lambda_0=0$, or with a predefined
rejection (partial recirculation), $\lambda_0\neq0$. For these regimes, the
desalination system operates in such a way that the mixing tank, initially with a volume
of brackish water of $V_{mix,0}=100$~L, has its mixture consumed by the DCMD module,
as distillate is produced over time.

Additionally, to analyze the influence of the external heat exchangers on the system
performance it is sufficient to vary their effectiveness ($\varepsilon_{HX1}$ and
$\varepsilon_{HX2}$). By choosing $\varepsilon_{HX1}=0$ and/or $\varepsilon_{HX2}=0$,
the heat exchangers are not included in the analysis.

In batch and semi-batch operation modes, over each process cycle, the distillate is
continuously removed from the system, and the remaining solution that leaves the MD
module, with higher salinity, is directed to the feed tank ($\lambda=0$). In the batch
operation process, there is no water pumping from the well ($\dot{V}_{well}=0$) between
recycling, so the volume of the solution in the tank decreases, and the salt concentration
increases over time. In the semi-batch operation cycle, the volume of the mixing tank is
kept constant, by continuously adding fresh feed water from the well to the system, at
the same rate at which distillate is removed ($\dot{V}_{well}=\dot{V}_{jwl}$).

In both operation processes, the permeate production rate and the heat-transfer
rate decrease as the feed stream of the MD module becomes increasingly saturated with
salts. After the salinity of the solution in the mixing tank reaches a limiting value,
the high-salinity brine is flushed-out ($\lambda$ is set to 1) until the minimum allowable volume
is reached. Then, the well pump refills the tank from the well ($\dot{V}_{well}=\dot{V}_s$) until the minimum concentration is reached, and the cycle restarts.

In the continuous operation regime, a fixed operational brine salinity is specified at
the MD module exit. Therefore, after the initial transients have elapsed and the solution
that leaves the module is at the required salinity (reaching the steady-state regime), it
must be continuously withdrawn from the system at a specific fraction rejection ($\lambda$)
that depends on the MD module performance. The make-up water from the well
($\dot{V}_{well}$) is simultaneously added to the tank at a proportional flow rate that
maintains the salinity of the stream at the MD module exit, which is associated with the corresponding
rejection fraction.The rejection fraction and the volumetric flow rate from the well are obtained from
the following salt and total-mass balances applied to the mixing tank:

\begin{equation}
\lambda
=
\frac{
\left(f_{3}-f_{2}\right)\dot{V}_{\mathrm{well}}
+
\left(f_{3}-f_{8}\right)\dot{V}_{7}
}{
\left(f_{2}-f_{8}\right)\dot{V}_{7}
}
\label{eq:l}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\dot{V}_{well}
=
\dot{V}_{jwl}
+
\lambda
\left(\dot{V}_s-\dot{V}_{jwl}\right)
\label{eq:Vwell}
\end{equation}

To simulate a steady-state condition with no recirculation, it is sufficient to set
$\lambda=\lambda_0=\lambda_1=1$ and $\dot{V}_{well}=\dot{V}_s$ all the time
during the simulations. In this situation, all retentate leaving the DCMD module is
rejected and the system is continuously fed by the well at constant feed flow rate and
salinity.As will be shown, if this mode of operation is mandatory, as in the case studied 
by \citet{Sampaio2022}, the use of the second heat exchanger (HX-2) to recover heat from 
the retentate stream before it is flushed out leads to an improvement in the performance
of the desalination system.

Because these operating modes are inherently transient, the distillate is continuously 
withdrawn from the system, while the concentrated stream leaving the MD module, whose 
salinity progressively increases, is either recirculated to the mixing tank or discharged
from the system, depending on the value of $\lambda$. In both cases, the use of HX-2 to recover 
thermal energy from the retentate stream before its discharge improves the overall energy efficiency 
of the desalination system, as demonstrated later.

In the volume-controlled mixing-tank operating mode, predefined minimum and maximum tank volumes are 
established as operational limits. As distillate is produced, the salinity of the solution in the mixing
tank, $c_{s,\mathrm{mix}}(t)$, continuously increases, whereas the liquid volume, $V_{\mathrm{mix}}(t)$, 
decreases. When the minimum allowable volume, $V_{\min}$, is reached, brackish water from the well is 
supplied to the tank until the maximum predefined volume, $V_{\max}$, is restored. This cycle is repeated 
throughout the desalination process. If the solution in the mixing tank reaches a predefined maximum operating 
salinity, the tank contents are completely discharged, corresponding to $\lambda = 1$. The tank is then
refilled with brackish well water until the initial volume, $V_{\mathrm{mix},0}$, is restored.

For an intuitive understanding of how the operational and configurational decisions
affect energy efficiency, \citet{Christie2020}, based on \citet{Lin2014}, introduced
the critical mass-flow-rate ratio, $\alpha^*$, to define the theoretical condition for
the maximum water-recovery factor in steady-state, single-pass DCMD systems,
$R_{\max}$:

\begin{equation}
\alpha^*
=
\left.
\frac{\dot{m}_s\,c_{p,s}}
{\dot{m}_l\,c_{p,l}}
\right|_{\mathrm{critical}}
\label{eq:alpha-star}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\alpha
=
\frac{\dot{m}_s\,c_{p,s}}
{\dot{m}_l\,c_{p,l}}
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_{\max}^{FLR}
>
R_{\max}^{DLR}
\label{eq:Rmax}
\end{equation}

For $\alpha<\alpha^*$, the module operates in the DLR regime. Conversely, for
$\alpha>\alpha^*$, the module operates in the FLR regime.

Overall, these results indicate that a balance must be sought between specific
energy consumption and water recovery, since a trade-off exists between these
metrics for the same maximum operating salinity. Regarding energy consumption,
the batch mode presents the best performance, followed by the semi-batch,
steady-state, and continuous modes, in agreement with the literature
\citep{SwaminathanLienhard2018}. Regarding water recovery,
Figure~\ref{fig:rr} indicates that semi-batch operation provides the highest
recovery ratio, followed by the volume-control and batch modes.

be the preferred alternative for brackish-water desalination because it provides
the best compromise when minimizing well-water consumption is the main
objective.

\section{Conclusions}

The present work developed a mathematical modeling and computer simulation of a DCMD
desalination system with double heat recovery from the distillate stream and the feed
stream, together with total or partial recirculation of the retentate. The main goal was
to employ the developed code to identify the best scenarios for operation of the system
in terms of maximizing its thermal efficiency while simultaneously minimizing the use of
brackish water from wells, seeking optimized production of the amount of distilled water.

The results show that heat recovery from the distillate stream already produces a
significant increase in the energy efficiency of the desalination system, constituting a
mandatory condition for the operation of DCMD-based systems. Beyond this, the use of an
additional heat exchanger to recover heat from the retentate stream further improves the
energy efficiency of the process, an effect that is especially pronounced when the system
operates in the FLR condition with a mass flow rate near the critical mass flow rate.
Under these circumstances, peaks in $GOR$ values and lows in $SEC$ values can be observed
when the well and an external heat exchanger coupled to its stream operate simultaneously.
Double heat recovery and partial recirculation of the retentate therefore play an
important role in the competitiveness of the membrane distillation process compared to
other forms of desalination, particularly in long operation processes.

The system behavior also proved sensitive to two operational factors. The amount of heat
supplied to the system is strongly impacted by the rejection fraction of the retentate
stream, and the operating mode itself has a strong influence on both the energy
consumption and the water recovery of the DCMD desalination system. In this regard, the
lowest specific thermal energy cost is associated with the batch operation mode of the
desalination plant. However, in regions subject to hydrologic seasonal stress, the results
indicate that the semi-batch mode could be the preferred choice for brackish water saving.
The selection of an operating mode must consequently be guided by site-specific
information, such as where the system is to be implemented and what the local availability
of energy and brackish water salinity are.

Extension to other desalination MD methods, such as AGMD and V-AGMD, is currently under
development, in order to simulate the entire experimental TRL-6 desalination unit installed
in the LabMEMS/UFRJ.
